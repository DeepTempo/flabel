# The flabel label corpus, as of 2026-08-28

**Read this before using the corpus as ground truth.** Revision 2, after a review of revision 1 found
that it was honest about three things and quietly reassuring about eight others. The specific way a
document like this fails is by leaving a reader believing the corpus has been *verified*, when what
has been verified is that the pipeline did not lose or corrupt anything. **Nothing here is evidence
that a label is correct.**

## What it is

24 captures, labelled into the `flabel` BigQuery dataset. `blfile` (bare, no flags) composes **880
flows**, each carrying a `gs://` origin.

| | flows | what it means |
| :-- | --: | :-- |
| tier 1 only | 400 | the NGFW's threat log flagged it, and **no tier-2 label exists for that flow** |
| **both tiers** | **8** | both flagged the same flow, from two independent runs |
| tier 2 only | 472 | Suricata flagged it, and **no tier-1 label exists for that flow** |

**The two tiers agree on 8 of 880 flows.** Those 8 are also the entire real-world exercise of the
cross-tier merge rule (#144), which had only fixtures behind it before this phase.

**Where these captures came from is not recorded here, and it needs to be.** `docs/prd.md` Q11
restricts flabel to *publicly-published captures with documented origin and licence*, and states that
pointing it at internal or customer traffic is a gate to answer first. The captures carry internal
addresses, DNS names, HTTP URIs and TLS server names. Nobody has written down which category they
fall in. **Answer that before any model trained on this corpus goes anywhere.**

## The absence of a label is never a negative

Revision 1 said, of the 17 two-tier captures, that a flow with only a tier-2 source "means the
firewall looked and did not flag it." **That was wrong, and it was the most consequential sentence in
the document.** `docs/prd.md` §3 is explicit: *"Not an assertion of benign. flabel labels malicious
flows. It does not and cannot certify that any flow is safe."* Revision 1 correctly forbade using
tier-1 absence as a signal *across* the corpus and, by explaining it that way, implicitly licensed it
*within* the 17.

What the absence actually means is *a tier-1 run over this capture produced no label on this flow*.
Measured across the 17 authoritative tier-1 runs:

- **11 unmatched detections.** Eleven times the firewall *did* detect something and it never became a
  label on a flow. For those flows, "the firewall looked and did not flag it" is simply false.
- **4 of 17 runs set `ambiguous_flow_match`** — a detection that matched more than one flow.
- `coverage.unmatched_ratio` is **present on all 880 records**, not null. Revision 2 claimed it was
  null for the 17 two-tier captures; that was taken from a review's speculation and never checked.
  Read it — and read each run's own `counts.unmatched` in its verbatim block under `runs[]`.

**So absence of a tier-1 source is not a negative label, in either population.** The difference
between the two populations below is real and still matters — but it is a difference in *what was
attempted*, not in *what was cleared*.

## The corpus has two populations, and a record cannot tell you which

- **17 captures carry tier 1 *and* tier 2** — a tier-1 replay was attempted over them.
- **7 captures carry tier 2 only** — they have never been replayed past the device at all.

Distinguish them **per capture, not per flow**: take `origin.capture_sha256`, find every `runs[]`
entry with that `capture_sha256`, and union their `supplies`. If `1` is in that union, a tier-1 run
was authoritative for the capture.

**Do not use the record's own `origin.run_ids`.** That map is per *flow* — it names only the tiers
that contributed to that flow — so a tier-2-only flow reads `{"2": ...}` in both populations and
cannot tell them apart. In the store the equivalent is
`SELECT capture_sha256 FROM flabel.authoritative_runs WHERE tier = 1`.

This reads *authority*, not examination: a capture whose tier-1 run was excluded (§4.5) or unattested
(§2.4) would also present as never-replayed. Neither is true today. §6.4's own standard — that
"recoverable with effort by cross-referencing three fields" is weaker than a field — says this
arrangement is substandard, and it is right. `coverage.tiers_examined` (#184) is the fix, and it is
not built.

## Toolchain divergence is structural — but the field I warned about is empty

Revision 2 said `flow.ja4` was "close to a perfect predictor" of which tier labelled a flow.
**Measured, and it is not: `ja4`, `ja4s` and `server_name` are null on all 880 flows, tier 2
included.** Not one labelled flow in this corpus carries TLS enrichment — see the protocol mix below,
which is why. The warning was asserted rather than counted, and counting it killed it.

**The mechanism behind it is real and worth keeping.** `merge._one_flow` takes the *entire* `flow`
struct from the **lowest** authoritative tier's row, so on a mixed-tier flow every struct field —
`ja4`, `ja4s`, `server_name`, `zeek_uid`, `ts_last` — is the tier-1 toolchain's value, whatever tier 2
computed. Today that leaks nothing because the fields are empty everywhere. The next toolchain
divergence on a field that *is* populated would leak, and installing a tool on the box does not fix
it. Re-measure per tier before using any struct field as a model feature.

`threat-name` is the same hazard in the label array and is live: it is tier-1-only, so
`--label threat-name` silently yields a 408-flow, 17-capture corpus.

## What this corpus must not be used for

Ordered by how likely the mistake is to produce a model that scores well and does not work.

- **Split by capture, never by flow.** 880 flows from 24 captures on one network over three weeks,
  and **one capture supplies 41% of them** (top three: 58%). A random flow-level split puts the same
  hosts, subnets and repeated activity on both sides, so what you measure is memorisation. Hold out
  whole captures, report per-capture, and treat any metric from a random split as void. With 24
  captures and one dominant, expect wide variance between folds — **that variance is the signal, not
  noise to average away.** This is also the instruction that makes every other hazard here *visible
  in your own numbers*: they all show up as a gap between a random split and a held-out-capture one.
- **Know what the traffic is before you model it. 52% of the corpus is port 22**, and the top four
  ports are 69% of it; 585 flows are TCP and 295 UDP, and **no flow carries TLS enrichment at all**.
  This is not a general malicious-traffic corpus — it is dominated by one service. A model trained
  here learns that service.
- **Do not use raw `flow.ip_lo`/`ip_hi` or `server_name` as features.** Every capture is from one
  network, so an address is close to a proxy for "which capture is this" and the model learns the
  proxy. It also puts internal addressing into the weights, which is a copy of this data outside the
  access question at the end of this document. Derive from them if you must; do not pass them
  through.
- **Drop the flows whose sources are *all* `indicator-reference` — not the source entries.** An
  `indicator-reference` label marks a flow that merely *referenced* a malicious indicator: the flow
  to your own resolver or proxy, not the malicious activity. `docs/prd.md` rates this High/High and
  its entire mitigation is that a consumer can exclude them. Measured: **711 source entries `direct`,
  177 `indicator-reference`, and 177 of 880 flows (20%) carry nothing but `indicator-reference`.**
  Test the whole `sources[]` array — it is a union across runs, so a flow can carry both bases, and
  such a flow is `direct`. **Dropping them is not free:** all 177 are tier-2-only, so the corpus goes
  to 703 and tier-1-bearing flows rise from 46% to 58%.
- **Check `sources[].direction` before using src/dst roles as features.** `docs/prd.md` measured that
  19.5% of the ruleset is destination-anchored with an unconstrained source, so a rule can fire on
  *our reply* to an unsolicited inbound packet while its `threat` text describes an outbound
  connection. Measured here: **711 `to_server`, 177 `to_client`** — and the 177 `to_client` are
  exactly the 177 indicator-reference entries, which is what you would expect if they are response
  legs. `direction` is Suricata's frame of reference for the matching packet, not the flow struct's
  orientation.
- **Do not redistribute a collection, or train for a commercial product, without checking licences.**
  `sources[].threat` is verbatim rule text and `sources[].licence` records its terms. Measured:
  **462 entries CC-BY-SA-4.0 (share-alike — it propagates to derivative works), 408
  `proprietary:vendor-signature (not redistributed)`, 18 MIT.** Unlike a per-run `labels.json`,
  **`blfile` writes no `NOTICE` beside a collection** — there is no notice generation in
  `src/flabeldb/` at all, so attribution is yours to produce. Note also that PRD Q3 (JA4+ under FoxIO
  1.1, non-commercial) is still open; it does not bite this corpus, whose JA4 fields are all null.
- **Do not join across runs on `flow.zeek_uid`, and do not guess which run it belongs to.** §3.2
  measured that Zeek's `uid` is positional, so a uid used against the wrong run's `conn.log` returns
  *a different real flow* rather than an error. Join corpus records to each other on
  `(origin.capture_sha256, flow_key)`. To reach `conn.log`, the uid is valid **only** in the run
  named by `origin.run_ids[str(best_tier)]` — the merge takes the flow struct from the lowest
  authoritative tier, so on the 17 two-tier captures that is the tier-1 run, not the Suricata one.
- **`threat` and `classtype` are not a taxonomy.** `threat` is verbatim rule `msg:` text on tier 2
  and a device threat identifier on tier 1, so any class built from it is partly a tier label;
  `classtype` is null on roughly 13% of admitted rules and `threat` carries the sentinel `"unnamed"`.
  flabel deliberately declines to promote tier-2 `msg` to a threat name (#138); decline too.
- **Report results separately by `admission_basis`; do not use it as a trust weight.** Measured:
  **462 wholesale, 408 device-policy, 18 metadata-filter** — so of the tier-2 evidence, all but 18
  entries come from sources with no per-rule gate. A flow can carry more than one basis, so reduce
  per flow before grouping. No false-positive rate has been measured for any of the three, so use
  this to see whether your errors concentrate, not as a ranking.
- **This corpus is positives only**, and a collection document contains no unlabelled flows at all.
  If you source negatives from these captures, "unlabelled" is not "benign" — see *The absence of a
  label is never a negative*. Known lower bound on contamination there: 11 tier-1 detections that
  never became a label, plus flows refused at ingest for an unnameable transport (uncounted).
- **`port_lo`/`port_hi` are not ports on ICMP rows** (§3.2) — they carry type and code.

## Precision is unmeasured for the whole corpus, not just half of it

The pre-Phase-4 baseline was 408 flows, all tier 1 — though bare `blfile` could select **none** of
them, for want of a recorded origin. This corpus is **472 of 880 (54%) tier-2-only**, and 480 of 880
(55%) carry at least one tier-2 source.

Tier 2's come from snapshot `682c24ae30631504`, 83,964 rules, including wholesale-admitted sources
with no per-rule gate — `pawpatrules` among them, which `docs/prd.md` calls the broadest-scope and
least-vetted admitted source, admitted with its false-positive risk knowingly accepted.

**But tier 1 is not vetted either.** A PANW threat log is also an automated verdict, from an
auto-updating content release, with no canary and no per-rule gate a consumer can inspect. The two
tiers differ in *why* you might trust them. **Neither has been measured.**

The false-positive control is a split, and neither half covers this corpus (#88). The half running on
every build is three synthetic rules against a 14-packet capture. The real half is
`.github/workflows/feeds.yml`, daily: nine feeds fetched live, a real snapshot built, a benign corpus
labelled against it, with `ci.yml` refusing a push if it has been dark for seven days. That is a
genuine standing review — but it builds a **fresh** snapshot each day and has therefore never run
against `682c24ae30631504`. Its runs either side of this corpus: 2026-08-29 success, 2026-08-30
**failure**, 2026-08-31 success. The 08-30 failure is unexplained and unexamined.

A measured false-positive rate remains an explicit PRD non-goal.

## What was checked, and what checking it does not tell you

Every row is about **pipeline integrity** — that nothing was lost, corrupted, or produced by a moving
toolchain. **None of it is evidence that a label is correct.**

| | |
| :-- | :-- |
| toolchain | uniform across the 24 **authoritative** tier-2 runs: Suricata 8.0.6, Zeek 8.2.1, editcap 4.6.6, ja4 v0.18.8, config digest `4ecf7728`. A 25th proving run is superseded and supplies nothing. The **400 tier-1 flows came from a different, August toolchain.** |
| ruleset | `rules_loaded == total_admitted` (83,964) on every tier-2 run. A completeness check on the *load*, not a quality check on admission. |
| captures | the 7 new captures read `input_status: complete` — no truncation, no discarded link types, zero warnings — including `capture_2026-07-21` at 966,416 packets, which failed the **replay** path twice on `tcpprep` and is tier-2-only for a reason rather than by omission. |
| origins | the store *recorded* a URI **the operator asserted**. §6.1 is explicit that flabel does not verify the URI holds the hashed bytes, and `flabel-run` reuses an already-staged local file with no digest check while setting `--source-uri` regardless (#179, open). Digests were checked by hand across bucket, staging and store for all 24 before the batches. |
| tier-1 preservation | the same 408 flows as before, identical tier-1 source entries, 0 lost / 0 gained / 0 changed. Proves nothing was destroyed. Says nothing about correctness. |
| cross-tier join | 8 joined on `flow_key`, 400 with no 5-tuple match in tier 2, **0** cases of one 5-tuple yielding two keys. That excludes the hazard that mattered — `flow_key` drifting between the two toolchains. It does **not** exclude a flow the tier-2 Zeek never produced a `conn` record for, nor a tier-2 detection refused at ingest for an unnameable transport (§3.2, #96). |
| archive | `reconcile_store.py` agrees on all 50 runs. **Cardinality only** — it compares counts, not label content. |
| exclusions | `run_exclusions` is empty: no run has been retracted. |
| reproduction | the **pre-operation 408-flow baseline** rebuilds identically against the post-operation store, over records, excluding `built_at`, `builder` and the derived `selection` counts (§6.5). The current 880-flow corpus has not been pinned to a document, so it has not been reproduced. |

## What is not

- **This is not a pinned artifact.** "The corpus" is what bare `blfile` emits against the live store
  on 2026-08-28. Supersession orders on `finished_at`, **not** on ruleset recency (§5.1, §4.5; §9
  leaves this open), so a later run pinned to an older snapshot would take over a tier. Exclusions
  can retract a run and are not reproduced by a rebuild (§5.5). P4-6's outstanding `--both` run will
  supersede tier 1 on one capture. **Build a `labels-collection` document and keep it** — it pins the
  run set and `supplies`, which is what makes `--rebuild` a function of the document plus the store.
- **Not a claim that unlabelled flows are benign.** See above; this is the one that matters most.
- **No false-positive rate.** Not measured, a PRD non-goal.
- **The store holds more than the corpus.** 2,362 `flow_labels` rows across 50 runs; the corpus is 880
  merged flows. The remainder is non-authoritative. Checked: it is almost entirely **one capture
  labelled five times** — `capture_2026-07-10` holds 352 authoritative rows and 1,430 superseded
  across 4 runs, each superseded run holding roughly what its successor holds. So supersession has
  *not* eroded tier-1 knowledge; the per-run averages merely look alarming because captures differ
  100-fold in label count.
- **Flows dropped at ingest leave no trace in the store.** §3.2 refuses a row for any flow whose
  transport is not `tcp`, `udp` or `icmp` (ESP, SCTP, GRE — #96), and `runs` has no column for the
  refusal count. Unmeasured for these captures.
- **The 35 `unmatched` rows are not all reachable from this corpus.** Only **11** belong to
  authoritative runs, all of them tier 1; the other 24 sit in superseded runs and no current label
  is affected by them.
- **Duplicate `SourceEntry` values are unbounded** (#58), so a composed `sources` list can carry
  repeats.

## Who may read the dataset — the gate was missed

**`docs/PLAN-phase-4.md` required this be settled or deferred in writing *before* P4-4. Nothing was
written, and P4-4 ran.** The store went from one tier-2 run to 24 captures of non-anonymous network
metadata, and the archive gained a per-flow `eve.json` for each.

This paragraph is that omission recorded after the fact. It is **not** a deferral — a deferral is a
decision taken before acting. The data has landed and the exposure is live; what is written down here
is that it was accepted by default, and the decision is still owed.

No new grant was made by this phase, and none was needed: **the exposure changed because the data did,
not because the access did.** The dataset's access posture and the open questions around it are
tracked in #161 and #162, and the operational detail is deliberately kept out of this public file.

**Before any external consumer receives a `labels-collection`, and before read access is granted to
anyone new, that decision has to be made properly.**
